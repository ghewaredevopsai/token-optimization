Tier 1

Choosing the model,
by use case

Six criteria, three archetypes, and a way of deciding that survives the roster changing next month — because it places the task, not the model.

5 slides · ~10 minutes Lab 1 follows · one matrix row you would defend Trainer: Rajesh Gheware

The grid

Six criteria. Note what is shaded and what is not.

The cells are shaded, not numbered, and that is deliberate. Any number written here is wrong within a month, and a slide that ages makes the whole deck untrustworthy. The shape — which way each row runs — has been stable for two years.

The residency row is the one that changes conversations in a bank. People arrive assuming the cheap models are the ones that can be hosted where they need them, and that is not a rule at all - it depends entirely on which vendor and which contract. Do not let the row be read as a tier property.

The frontier

Place the task, not the model

Nothing on this chart is a model name, so it survives the roster changing. When a new model lands you do not redraw the chart — you move the diagonal, and every task keeps its place.

Ask the room to place two of their own tasks before you move on. Most teams have never articulated the vertical axis, and it is the one that decides: "what does it cost us if this is quietly wrong" is a different question from "is this hard".

Evidence, not reputation

Write the guess down before you open the data

How this normally goes

  • Run three models on one task.
  • Read the three answers.
  • Prefer the one you expected to prefer.
  • Call it a benchmark.

With one run per cell, and no prediction recorded, you cannot tell a result from a preference.

What the lab does instead

  • Predict which archetype wins on your three commonest tasks.
  • Write the prediction down. Sixty seconds.
  • Then open the recorded runs.
  • Count how many predictions survived.

Typically one of three. That number is the finding, and it is about you, not the models.

This module does not re-run the three-model comparison. That lab exists, it works, and it belongs to the Copilot as Assistant module. Here we analyse the output of one — which is also the only version that works for a room whose model picker shows nothing but Auto.

Pre-registration is the whole trick and it is worth naming as such. It is the difference between evidence and confirmation, it costs a minute, and it is the single most portable habit in this tier - it works on any decision where somebody already has an opinion.

The row you would defend

A selection rule has to say what it is not

Not defensible

Use the reasoning model for
anything important.

"Important" is every task, to the person doing it. This costs you the frontier rate on docstrings.

Defensible

Unit tests and multi-file changes:
default tier.
Money, dates or boundaries: reasoning,
high effort, and read the diff.
Everything else: Auto.
Revisit when the roster changes.
Lab 1 now: write your prediction, open the recorded runs, and produce one row of that guide you would be willing to defend to the person who disagrees with you. Record how many of your three predictions survived contact with the data.

The "revisit when the roster changes" line is not filler. A selection rule with no expiry is how teams end up on last year's defaults; making the review trigger explicit is the cheapest governance in the module.
